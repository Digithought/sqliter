# Status: the measurement is DONE. This ticket is the write-up and the decision.

The predecessor (`3.4-bench-leveldb-read-cost`) built the benchmark, ran it, and hit its
token budget before recording the outcome. The benchmark code is complete, reviewed against
the harness, and working — do not rebuild it. **The numbers below are already measured; do
not re-measure to get started.**

## What landed

`packages/quereus/bench/suites/store.bench.mjs` gained a third group, `READ COST ON REAL
DISK`, with two benchmarks — `leveldb-read-cost-20k` and `leveldb-read-cost-200k`. Each
seeds N rows of 200-byte values into a fresh LevelDB temporary directory through
`KVStore.batch()`, over real `encodeCompositeKey` integer keys, then times three arms per
round against that one dataset:

- **sequential** — a full `iterate()` draining every value. The 1.0 denominator.
- **batched random point read** — 1 000 random keys through `getMany`, paged at the imported
  `ROW_RESOLUTION_BATCH`.
- **single-key seek** — 1 000 *different* random keys, one `iterate({gte, lt, limit: 1})`
  each, drained and closed.

All three share one benchmark per size deliberately (the harness forks a process per
benchmark, so three rows would compare three medians with three different cache histories).
`teardown` takes the median per arm across rounds, discarding the first, and prints the two
ratios to stdout. Both rows are `informational: true`; the suite declares no ratio guards, so
they enter no verdict.

Verified working:

- `node packages/quereus/bench/run.mjs --filter store/leveldb-read-cost` with the opt-in
  unset — both rows print `skipped — disk-backed rows are opt-in and advisory — set
  QUEREUS_BENCH_LEVELDB=1 to run them`, marked `informational`.
- The same with `QUEREUS_BENCH_LEVELDB=1` — both rows complete, both ratio blocks print.
  Total wall clock 10.3 s for the pair; the 200k row's own median is 1 001 ms per round, far
  clear of the 120 s per-benchmark timeout.
- `loadSuite('store.bench.mjs')` reports 27 benchmarks, 0 ratio guards, and exactly the two
  new names as informational.

The suite file header was updated to describe three groups instead of two.

## The numbers (AMD Ryzen AI 9 HX 370, 24 cores, 31 GB, Windows 11 Pro 26200, NVMe, node v24.2.0, measured 2026-08-19)

```
leveldb-read-cost-20k: 20000 rows x 200-byte values, median of 13 rounds
  sequential   full iterate              0.002514 ms/row   (the 1.0 denominator)
  batched      getMany, paged            0.003156 ms/row   pointRead          1.26
  single-seek  iterate, one window       0.047205 ms/key   seekPositioning   18.78

leveldb-read-cost-200k: 200000 rows x 200-byte values, median of 7 rounds
  sequential   full iterate              0.004442 ms/row   (the 1.0 denominator)
  batched      getMany, paged            0.006399 ms/row   pointRead          1.44
  single-seek  iterate, one window       0.069098 ms/key   seekPositioning   15.55
```

Harness row medians: 20k = 100.46 ms, spread 28.5% (marked `unstable`, 11 samples);
200k = 1 001.16 ms, spread 5.0% (5 samples). The row median is the cost of one whole round
and is not the interesting number — but the 20k row's 28.5% spread does mean its per-arm
medians are noisier than the 200k row's, and that belongs in the write-up.

## Reading the result — the split outcome

**`pointRead` is near parity.** 1.26 and 1.44 against a 1.0 default. Real, small.

**`seekPositioning` is far from parity.** 15.55 and 18.78 against a 0.5 default — a factor
of roughly 31 to 38. The cause is visible in the raw milliseconds: a batched read costs
~3.2 µs per key while a single-key `iterate` costs ~47 µs, so roughly 44 µs is fixed
per-iterator setup and teardown (snapshot, native iterator, close) that `getMany` amortizes
across a whole page and a per-seek-key window cannot. `StoreTableScan.scanMultiSeek` really
does open one iterator per seek key over the index store, so the shape being priced is the
shape the engine runs.

A cross-check that the direction is right, not just the magnitude: at these rates a
1 000-key multi-seek over a 10 000-row table costs ~47 ms while the full scan it would
displace costs ~25 ms. The scan wins. Under the current `seekPositioning: 0.5` the planner
prices the break-even at roughly N seek keys (`N/(2S)`), i.e. it would pick the seek. So the
parity default is not merely imprecise here — it points the wrong way at high key counts.

**The honest caveat, which the decision has to weigh.** These ratios were measured at the
key-value layer with no engine above them, deliberately (engine overhead is roughly equal on
both sides and would compress every ratio toward 1.0). But the planner's own 1.0 unit is a
scan row *including* engine work. Adding, say, 2 µs of per-row engine cost to both sides
moves 18.8 to about 11 — still an order of magnitude from 0.5, so the conclusion survives,
but the exact number to declare is not simply the printed ratio.

  Moving it from 0.5 to ~15 shifts the seek/scan break-even by roughly 30×, on the backend

  which branch is taken: the table above, the date (2026-08-19), the machine, both dataset
  sizes, and the block-cache caveat. That is what stops the next person re-asking.
- **Cross-reference `debt-leveldb-cost-profile-measurement`** rather than duplicating its
  reasoning, and do NOT close it from here — the decision is a separate, deliberate act. It
  now reduces to reading the numbers above and choosing.
- **Update `docs/benchmarking.md`.** Counts move: the intro says 71 benchmarks across five
  suites, the *What is measured* table's `store` row says 25, and *The `store` suite* section
  states the 11 / 14 split of its halves. It is now 73 and 27, in three groups (11 / 14 / 2).
  Add prose for the read-cost group: the three arms, that the row median is one whole round
  and not the interesting number, that the ratios are printed rather than reported as
  counters (a wall-clock ratio has no noise floor, so a counters block would report a change
  every run), and the block-cache-versus-page-cache caveat below. Note the rows are
  informational and enter no gate.
- **State the cold/warm claim precisely, wherever it is written down.** The two sizes do NOT
  separate page-cache-cold from page-cache-warm; there is no portable way to drop the OS page
  cache from Node. What they separate is `classic-level`'s 8 MB **block cache**: 20 000 rows
  (~4 MB) fits inside it, 200 000 rows (~40 MB) does not, so the large size's random reads go
  out to the filesystem — which on a warm machine usually means the OS page cache, not the
  disk. A claim of "cold" that is really "block-cache-miss, page-cache-hit" is worse than no
  claim.
